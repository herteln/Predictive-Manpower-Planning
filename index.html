Business problem

Technical-support organizations need the right number of people, with the right product and technical knowledge, available at the right time. Understaffing can affect response times and service levels; overstaffing can waste capacity. The long-term goal of this project is to use historic ticket patterns to support forward-looking manpower planning.

Research questions

1. Which and how many employees may be needed for upcoming shifts, based on product or product group, expected workload, and required knowledge?

2. If a new product or release becomes supported, what additional staffing and product knowledge may be required to absorb the extra work?

Why it matters

Support workflows leave digital traces in ticketing systems. Those event-like records can reveal demand patterns, workload, routing, SLA outcomes, interactions, and customer-feedback signals. In a mature solution, those signals could be combined with employee skills, certifications, availability, and shift data to improve workforce planning.

Dataset

The project uses the Technical Support Dataset from Kaggle. The notebook analyzes 2,330 ticket records and works with operational and categorical attributes such as status, priority, source, topic, agent group, agent name, SLA outcomes, agent interactions, survey results, product group, support level, and country.

Included signals

Status · Priority · Ticket source · Topic · Agent group · Agent · SLA performance · Agent interactions · Survey result · Product group · Support level · Country

Excluded in the initial notebook

Ticket ID, geographic coordinates, created time, expected SLA timestamps, first-response time, close time, and resolution time were removed before modeling.

Future improvement: selected time fields should be re-engineered rather than discarded, because time-of-day, weekday, seasonality, response duration, and resolution duration are directly relevant to workforce demand.

Validation finding: target leakage

These perfect scores should not be interpreted as evidence of perfect real-world prediction. In the supplied dataset, Good_Solved is 1 for every record with a non-missing Survey results value and 0 for every record where the survey result is missing. Because Survey results is also used as an input feature, the target can effectively be reconstructed from a predictor. A revised experiment should remove this leakage and redefine the target so it represents a future outcome that would not already be known at prediction time.

From classification to manpower planning

The strongest next step is to move from a ticket-quality classification exercise to a forecasting and resource-planning pipeline that directly predicts workload and staffing demand.

1. Redefine the target

Forecast ticket volume by day, shift, support level, product group, priority, or topic. This creates an outcome that directly supports staffing decisions.

2. Add workforce data

Combine ticket demand with employee skills, certifications, product knowledge, availability, shift calendars, productivity, and absence data.

3. Engineer time features

Use weekday, hour, month, seasonality, release periods, SLA durations, and lagged ticket volumes to capture recurring demand patterns.

4. Use forecasting models

Compare baseline time-series methods and machine-learning regressors for ticket-volume forecasts instead of relying only on binary classification.

5. Translate demand into staffing

Estimate required headcount from forecast workload, average handling time, target service levels, skill coverage, and productive hours per employee.

6. Validate operationally

Use time-based validation and compare forecasts against future periods, then evaluate both prediction error and business measures such as SLA coverage and staffing variance.
